The world flickered.

No, that wasn't correct.  

The world around him rapidly changed.  At first, the changes appeared to be random, but as the flashes began to slow down, it felt more like a few options were on a wheel of chance and spun.  As each option passed the "needle", the world flashed into existence.

He began to recognize patterns and was able to eventually distinguish three distinct worlds.

The first appeared to be a grimdark cityscape, complete with streetlights and police sirens.

The second was a forest, the trees were the color only a New England autumn could create.

The third was the strangest of all.  It was garish colors, clearly old animation quality.

The worlds spun around him, slowing.  Each frame hung for just a few more seconds as if the system were deciding which option fit him.

He was back in that Wesley Snipes vampire movie, black trenchcoat and overwrought sunglasses in the urban midnight.  This time he caught the whiff of city pollution and the stink of werewolves on the air.

"At least I'm not naked", he thought to himself.

Flash.  He was back in the woods.  He recognized this place.  It was the old Cub Scout camp that he attended years ago.  A path encircled a large pond in the middle that they would swim and canoe in.

Flash.  He was back in the cartoon.  This time he recognized the goofy, incompetent knight, the acerbic princess character and the poorly colored dragon. This was that old D&D cartoon from, when?  

Charlie felt his stomach clench as the flashing slowed.  

"Forest, Please the forest", he muttered like a mantra.  The vampire movie had freaked him out and the cartoon was from his childhood, a show that he barely remembered watching because it was the only thing on the TV at the time.

The system, or AI or whatever that snarky little text asshole was called, had proven that to him.  It could mute him, override his ability to talk. There was no response from the AI, but he knew where to go.  There had been a small "fort" on the path that surrounded the lake.  He and his friends would play zombie games when they had free time during the camp weeks.  Charlie was pretty sure that was where he was supposed to go next.

The fort was just as he remembered it.  Actually, it was almost too pristine.  The boards and paint looked fresh.

The twin doors between the towers opened for him and he entered the central room.  Weapons were mounted on plinths in a semi-circle.  Behind him, the doors closed, partly shutting off the rustling leaves and sounds of wildlife outside.

Two options grabbed Charlie's attention.  An axe sat in plain stand on top of the black plinth.  The other was a bow leaning in a similar stand with a quiver of arrows hanging from a peg on the front of the plinth.  The implication was obvious.  Charlie needed to pick one of the weapons.  And of the swords, hammers and other exotic weapons, the axe and bow called to him.

He moved towards the axe, it called to him.  Modern lumberjacks used heavy machinery and chainsaws more than the archaic axe, but something about the stout double-headed edged weapon appealed.

"Too bad it's so heavy," he muttered.  "That's going to be a bitch to swing around for any amount of time."

The plinth shimmered and the single heavy axe melted away to be replaced by twin hand-axes, hatchets really.  

"That's better."  Charlie said as he picked up both hatchets, one in each hand.  They were sleek weapons, the butts were slightly flared and curled to help his grip when he swung.  The balance was a bit off for throwing, but that was ok.  They weren't throwing axes.

About a mile later, he came across a bridge across a creek.  Two things stood out for him.  One, his memories didn't have a bridge crossing a creek along this path. It certainly wasn't this narrow, hand-built bridge across a steeply sided creek bed.

The second oddity was that the bridge was blocked by what appeared to be a large spider web.  It wasn't a normal spider web, the strands were far too thick, about the size of manilla cordage.  It covered the width of the bridge entirely and the only options were either through the webbing or off the side of the creek and up the other.

He approached the central point of the web and noticed the strands had been tied.  Not melded, like a real spider-web would have been, but tied in an intracate knot.  It certainly wasn't one that he had learned in Cub Scouts those decades ago or through what limited rigging his life as a lumberjack had instilled into him.

He touched the knot with a finger, expecting his hand to become stuck.  But, it wasn't sticky.  It felt like good stout rope, even with its gossamer appeareance.  He yanked at it, and the strands were steel taut and didn't budge, like the rigging in a child's climbing structure.

"Well, it was worth a shot."  He said as he pulled harder.  "It's obviously a puzzle, but how do I get across?"

The structure appearance and the intricate knot seemed to hint that he needed to climb through the strands.  A test of dexterity perhaps?  No, he wasn't going to fit through the gaps.  They were quite literally too thin for his body and the footing would have been trecherous.

He peered off the sides of the creekbed.  The water didn't appear to be deep here, but it was muddy and hard to judge.  It flowed slowly, without a ripple that would have indicated rocks below the surface.  What stopped him was the sheer edge of the opposite side.  He could easily jump down, but getting back up the other side would have been muddy if not impossible.

He tried to trace the curves and separate strands in the knot.  He wasn't the best at untying knots, his thick fingers tended to be less than dextrous at grasping strands and his mind had never really learned how to untie things.  He usually just cut the strings off the packages and the ribbons off his gifts rather than trying to untie them.

He paused a few moments and fingered the hatchet in his hand.

"Let's not over-think this", he muttered and swung one of his hatchets against the knot.  To his surprise, the thick strand parted cleanly with little effort.  The webbing fell away and the path was open
